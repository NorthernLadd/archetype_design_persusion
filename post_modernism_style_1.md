# Postmodernism Style 1: Vanna Venturi House

## The Building

Designed by architect Robert Venturi for his mother, Vanna, the Vanna Venturi House was completed in 1964 in Chestnut Hill, Philadelphia. Its familiar image of a house is immediately recognizable, but the details make that image feel deliberately unsettled. The building is widely discussed as an early landmark of postmodern architecture.

## What Makes Its Style Distinctive

- **A familiar house shape, made strange:** The broad, shallow gable-like front and chimney recall a simple, traditional house. Their scale and arrangement are exaggerated, so the facade reads almost like a graphic symbol rather than a straightforward expression of the rooms inside.
- **Symmetry that does not quite resolve:** The front appears balanced around a central axis, yet its pieces resist perfect symmetry. The entrance is recessed and offset, while the windows vary in size and placement. The viewer gets an expectation of order, followed by small but noticeable disruptions.
- **A broken, oversized pediment:** The roofline suggests a classical pediment, but its form is simplified and interrupted. It refers to architectural history without copying it literally, a characteristic postmodern move.
- **An arch used as a layered sign:** A large arch is set across the front, framing and emphasizing the entry area. It overlaps with the gable and other facade elements, creating a composition of shapes that feel both familiar and contradictory.
- **Contradiction between facade and plan:** The front is composed like a strong, almost symmetrical emblem, while the interior arrangement is more complex and asymmetrical. The outside does not simply reveal the inside; it stages a deliberate tension between the two.
- **Ordinary materials and domestic scale:** Rather than relying on sleek industrial abstraction, the house uses recognizable residential forms and materials. Its postmodern ideas are carried by proportion, reference, and composition.

## Why It Matters

Venturi challenged the modernist preference for visual purity and a single, consistent architectural expression. The house combines references to classical architecture and everyday domestic building, then alters those references with ambiguity, distortion, and asymmetry. It asks viewers to read the facade as both a real home and a composition of architectural symbols.

This approach is closely associated with Venturi's argument for complexity and contradiction in architecture. The Vanna Venturi House does not resolve its competing ideas into one clean statement; their coexistence is the point.

## Style at a Glance

| Feature | How it appears in the house |
|---|---|
| Historical reference | A simplified pediment and arch recall classical forms |
| Familiar symbolism | The facade evokes a recognizable image of a house |
| Contradiction | The apparently balanced front contains asymmetrical details |
| Complexity | Multiple overlapping shapes compete for attention |
| Postmodern character | Traditional references are quoted, altered, and recombined |
